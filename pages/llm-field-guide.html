In short

A field guide to how large language models actually work, built around instruments rather than diagrams. The tokenizer, the attention heatmap, the mixture-of-experts router, the sampler, the KV-cache and LoRA calculators, the context budget and the retrieval ranker all compute in the page, from the same formulas the surrounding prose describes.

Chapters 01–05 build the mechanism: tokens, attention and position, the stack and sparsity, sampling, and what long context costs. Chapters 06–07 cover where frontier models diverge and how weights are trained and adapted. Chapters 08–12 are the practice — prompting, context engineering, retrieval, tool loops and evals.

It is deliberately short on model names and prices, which go stale within months, and long on the axes that do not. Load-bearing numbers are cited; everything else is exposition and says so.

01Tokens and vectors

Before anything else happens, your text is chopped into tokens — chunks of a few characters — and each chunk is swapped for its row number in a fixed vocabulary. A model never sees your words. It sees a list of integers, and everything downstream is arithmetic on those integers.

The chopping is learned, not rule-based. Byte-pair encoding starts from raw bytes and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair until it has a vocabulary of the target size, typically 50,000 to 200,000 entries1. Frequent words survive as one token. Rare words shatter into pieces. This is why models are strangely bad at counting the letters in a word — they never saw the letters — and why the same sentence costs noticeably more in Romanian or Japanese than in English.

From integer to vector

The integer is only an address. It indexes into an embedding matrix — one learned vector per vocabulary entry — and that vector is what actually flows through the network. Meaning lives in direction and distance, not in the ID: two tokens with adjacent row numbers have nothing in common, while two tokens used in similar contexts end up pointing in similar directions.

What's real here

The splitting behaviour mirrors a real tokenizer, but the merge table is a stand-in built from a word list rather than learned from a corpus. The vectors are deterministic pseudo-random: a real embedding row is trained, and holds thousands of numbers rather than eight.

02Attention, and how position gets in

Every token asks a question, every token advertises an answer, and the strength of the match decides how much each one contributes to the next representation. Attention is a weighted average, and the weights are earned.

Each token's vector is projected three ways: a query (what am I looking for), a key (what do I offer), and a value (what I will hand over if chosen). Take the dot product of every query with every key, divide by the square root of the head dimension, softmax each row into probabilities, then mix the values with those probabilities2.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d ) · V
In wordsCompare every query against every key with a dot product, which is large when two vectors point the same way. Divide by √d, the square root of the head dimension, so the scores stay in a range softmax can work with. Turn each row of scores into weights that sum to one. Then take that weighted average of the value vectors.

The division by √d looks like a footnote and is not. Dot products of long vectors grow with dimension; without the scale, softmax saturates, one weight goes to nearly 1.0, and gradients die. Turn it off in the panel below and watch the matrix go binary.

Attention is order-blind, and has to be fixed

Notice what the formula does not contain: any notion of where a token sits. A dot product between a query and a key is identical whether the two tokens are adjacent or a thousand apart. Shuffle the input and every attention weight simply follows its token to the new position. On its own, this mechanism sees a bag, not a sentence.

So position has to be injected deliberately. The original transformer added fixed sinusoidal vectors to the embeddings — a different frequency per dimension, so every position gets a distinct signature and relative offsets stay expressible2. Learned absolute position embeddings were the next common choice. Both share a weakness: they do not extrapolate past the lengths seen in training.

Rotary position embedding is what essentially all current models use instead. Rather than adding anything to the vector, RoPE rotates the query and key by an angle proportional to their absolute position, within each two-dimensional slice of the head. Because the dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of their angles, the attention score becomes a function of relative distance without anyone storing a relative-position table3. That property is what makes context extension work: stretching or interpolating the rotation frequencies lets a model trained at one length operate beyond it, which is how a claim like “256K native, 1M by extrapolation”6 is built.

Why several heads

One head can only express one notion of relevance. Real blocks run dozens in parallel on slices of the vector, then concatenate the results. Switch heads above: same sentence, same arithmetic, different random projections, a completely different pattern. In a trained model those differences are not random — heads specialise into things like tracking the subject of a clause, matching brackets, or copying a rare name from earlier in the prompt.

What's real here

The arithmetic is the genuine mechanism: projections, dot products, √d scaling, causal masking, row-wise softmax and additive sinusoidal position. The weight matrices are seeded random rather than trained, so the patterns are structurally honest but semantically meaningless. The demo uses the older additive scheme rather than RoPE for one reason: additive position can be switched off in a single line, and being able to switch it off is the whole point of the toggle.

03The stack, and mixture of experts

A transformer is embarrassingly repetitive: one block, stacked dozens of times, each repetition nudging every token's vector a little further toward a representation from which the next word is obvious.

Inside a block: normalise, run multi-head attention, add the result back to the input; normalise again, run a feed-forward network, add that back too. Those add-backs are residual connections, and they are the reason depth works at all — each block writes an edit onto a shared stream rather than replacing it, so gradients have a clean path back to layer one2.

Attention moves information between tokens. The feed-forward network processes each token alone and holds most of the parameters, behaving like a large key-value store of learned patterns. Which is exactly why it became the target for sparsity.

Mixture of experts

Split that one fat feed-forward network into many smaller ones and put a small router in front that picks a handful per token. Capacity gets decoupled from cost: you buy more knowledge with memory instead of with arithmetic. DeepSeek-V3 is a clear published example — 671 billion total parameters with 37 billion activated per token, using 256 routed experts plus one always-on shared expert, of which eight routed experts fire for each token4.

Total capacity grew several-fold while the compute spent per token stayed roughly flat.

The catch

Routing is a discrete choice inside a differentiable system, which makes mixture-of-experts training unstable in specific ways. Experts collapse toward popularity, so load balancing needs deliberate handling — DeepSeek-V3 reports an auxiliary-loss-free strategy for exactly this4 — and serving requires spreading experts across several GPUs, because no single card holds them all.

04Sampling: from logits to a word

The final layer produces one raw score — a logit — for every entry in the vocabulary. Softmax turns those into probabilities. Then a sampler chooses, and that choice is not part of the model at all.

This split matters more than it sounds. Two “different” answers from the same model on the same prompt are usually the same distribution sampled twice. Determinism is a sampler setting, not a model property.

Reading the dials

Temperature divides the logits before softmax. Below 1 it sharpens and the leader pulls away; above 1 it flattens, and banana starts to have a real chance. Top-k keeps the k best candidates and renormalises. Top-p, or nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set whose probability sums to at least p, so it adapts: wide when the model is genuinely uncertain, narrow when it is not. Drag temperature to 2.0 with top-p at 1.00 and you can watch fluency dissolve in real time.

Where reasoning models fit

Test-time compute is a third scaling axis alongside training compute and architectural sparsity. A reasoning model spends many sampled tokens on intermediate work before its visible answer — same architecture, same sampler, just far more of it. You pay per call rather than once at training time, which is why “thinking” modes are priced and rate-limited differently.

05The KV cache, and what context costs

Generation happens one token at a time, and each new token attends to everything before it. Recomputing all those keys and values at every step would be absurd, so they are cached — and that cache is what actually limits how long a conversation can get. Long context is not a feature. It is a memory bill you pay every step.

cache_bytes = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × tokens × batch × bytes_per_value
In wordsThe leading 2 is one key and one value per position. Every term is a multiplier, and tokens is the one your users control.

Grouped-query attention, and why every model has it now

Notice that the cache scales with key/value heads, not query heads. Grouped-query attention exploits exactly that: keep all the query heads, but let groups of them share one set of keys and values7. Drag KV heads from 64 down to 8 above — an eightfold cut in cache for a small quality cost. Other approaches attack the same term differently; DeepSeek-V3 uses multi-head latent attention, compressing keys and values into a low-rank latent vector rather than reducing the head count4.

Prefill vs. decodeshape of each curve, not their sizes

prefill — reading the prompt; its attention term grows with the square of the length  ·  decode — writing each token, in proportion to length

Each series is scaled to its own maximum, so what you can read off this is curvature: one bows, the other is a ramp. The two heights in a single column are not comparable — prefill and decode cost different things, and any point where the bars happen to meet is an artifact of the units rather than a context length at which something real changes.

Two phases with different economics. Prefill reads your whole prompt in one parallel pass, and its attention term grows with the square of the length. Decode then emits tokens one at a time, cheap per token but bound by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic. A long prompt with a short answer is prefill-dominated; a short prompt with a long answer is decode-dominated. Prompt caching attacks the first, speculative decoding the second.

Why the agent bill surprises people

Conversation history is resent on every turn, so cost accumulates against the running total rather than the last message. Ten turns that each add 2K tokens do not cost 20K of input: turn one reads 2K, turn two reads 4K, and turn ten reads 20K, for about 110K altogether. Hold the window at a full 20K for all ten turns and it is 200K. Either way the bill grows with the square of the conversation, not with its length, which is why compaction and pruning pay off compounding rather than linearly.

06Where frontier models diverge

Everything in the previous chapters is common to all of them. The differences sit in four places — and unlike model names, context windows and prices, these four axes have stayed stable across several generations. They are what to reason about; the leaderboard is what to look up.

Axis 1 · Sparsity

Does the model activate all of its parameters for every token, or a fraction? Dense buys predictable latency and simpler serving. Sparse buys far more stored capacity per unit of inference compute, at the cost of holding every expert in memory anyway. The published trajectory of open models runs strongly toward sparse: 28% active for Mixtral 8x22B in April 20245, 5.5% for DeepSeek-V3 that December4, 7.3% for Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B in July 20256.

Axis 2 · Attention geometry

Nobody runs plain quadratic attention at very long context. The cache formula from the previous chapter has several terms, and each one has become a target. Reduce the key/value head count and you get grouped-query attention7. Compress keys and values into a low-rank latent and you get multi-head latent attention, as in DeepSeek-V34. Restrict which positions each token may attend to and you get one of the many sparse-attention patterns. Replace some attention layers with recurrent or state-space blocks and you get a hybrid. These are not interchangeable: they trade quality, cache size and implementation complexity differently, and a model's long-context behaviour is largely downstream of which one it picked.

Axis 3 · Advertised context versus context that works

This is the number most worth distrusting, and the reason is mechanical rather than commercial. Attention has to spread a fixed probability mass over every position, so precision on any one of them falls as the span grows.

The cleanest published demonstration is NoLiMa, which deliberately strips literal keyword overlap between the question and the buried fact, forcing associative retrieval rather than pattern matching. Across models claiming at least 128K of context, performance held up in short contexts and fell sharply as length grew; at 32K, most of the models tested scored at or below half their short-context baseline. GPT-4o, one of the stronger performers, dropped from a 99.3% baseline to 69.7% at 32K8.

The practical reading: treat an advertised window as a hard ceiling on what fits, not as a promise about what the model will reliably use. And note that the ranking depends on task shape — single-fact retrieval, multi-fact retrieval and long-range reasoning degrade at different rates — so a single “effective context” percentage is the wrong abstraction. Measure on your own task.

Axis 4 · Test-time compute

The newest axis, and the one that breaks cost estimates. A reasoning model spends tokens on intermediate work before answering, so the same nominal model can consume wildly different compute per request depending on how much of that it does. Qwen3-Coder's post-training used long-horizon reinforcement learning across many parallel environments specifically to train multi-turn tool interaction6 — capability that shows up at inference as more tokens spent, not as a bigger network.

What this chapter deliberately omits

An earlier draft carried a table of named current models with context windows and per-million-token prices. It has been removed. Those figures came from secondary comparison sites rather than model cards, and they go stale within months, which makes them exactly the wrong thing to fix in a document meant to last. The four axes above are stable. Check current model cards for the numbers.

07Weights, training and tuning

You have already met them. The embedding matrix in chapter 01, the query and key projections in chapter 02, the feed-forward layers in chapter 03 — those matrices are the weights. A “70B model” is 70 billion numbers, and training is the process of nudging each one until next-token prediction gets good.

Nothing in there is a fact in any retrievable sense. There is no row for your company's return policy, no lookup table of dates. Knowledge is smeared across billions of parameters as a side effect of compressing text well enough to predict it. That is why models can be simultaneously encyclopedic and confidently wrong, and why you cannot edit a fact out of one.

How a base model becomes an assistant

Pretraining alone produces something that continues text but will not reliably answer a question. Further stages fix that, and they are small by comparison. DeepSeek-V3's report describes exactly this shape: pretraining on 14.8 trillion tokens, followed by supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning stages4.

Three stages, wildly different budgets
StageScaleWhat it does
PretrainingTrillions of tokensPredict the next token over a very large corpus. Where the weights come from, and where essentially all the cost sits.
Supervised fine-tuningThousands to millions of pairsCurated instruction and response pairs. Teaches the base model to answer rather than autocomplete.
Reinforcement learningPreference or reward signalOptimise toward preferred responses, from human comparisons, model judgments or task reward. Where tone, refusals and format habits are set.

Most of what you experience as a model's “personality” — how it formats, when it hedges, what it declines — was installed in those later stages, on a data budget rounding to zero against pretraining. That is also why two labs can start from similar architectures and ship products that feel nothing alike.

Open weights, and what the phrase does not mean

Open weights means the trained numbers are downloadable: you can run the model on your own hardware, inspect it, quantize it, fine-tune it, and keep your data off someone else's API. It does not mean open source in the usual sense — training data and training code are almost never released, so the artifact is reproducible only in the way a compiled binary is.

The licences differ sharply and matter commercially. Some are genuinely permissive: Mistral released Mixtral 8x22B under Apache 2.0, describing it as the most permissive option and allowing use without restriction5, and Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B shipped under Apache 2.0 as well6. Others impose usage restrictions, user-count thresholds or naming requirements, which makes them open weights but not Apache-equivalent for a business. Read the licence file in the repository, not the announcement blog post.

The adaptation ladder

When a model does not do what you need, there are four rungs, and the discipline is to climb only as far as necessary. Most teams jump to fine-tuning when what they needed was retrieval.

RungChangesUse whenWrong for
PromptingNothing — context onlyThe behaviour is achievable, you just have not specified itRules that must hold across a thousand calls
RetrievalNothing — context onlyThe model lacks facts you ownStyle, format, or a domain vocabulary it has never seen
LoRA / adaptersA fraction of a percent of weightsYou need consistent style or format at low latency costTeaching genuinely new knowledge
Full fine-tuneAll weightsNew modality, new language, deep domain shiftAlmost everything else — expensive, and it forgets

The rule of thumb: fine-tuning teaches form, retrieval supplies facts. If your failure is “it does not know our SKUs,” no amount of tuning fixes it reliably — the model will learn to produce SKU-shaped strings, which is worse than not knowing. If your failure is “it will not stop writing like a press release,” tuning is exactly right.

Why LoRA works

Low-rank adaptation freezes every original weight and inserts a pair of thin matrices beside each target layer. Instead of updating a d×d matrix, you train a d×r and an r×d and add their product, so each adapted matrix costs 2·r·d trainable parameters instead of d². The adaptation is constrained to a low-rank subspace, which turns out to be plenty, because task adaptation is a much smaller change than the model itself9.

Quantization, and the thread back to chapter 05

Switch the base precision above from fp16 to fp4 and watch the LoRA figure fall by another factor of four. Quantization stores each weight in fewer bits, and the quality cost has fallen far enough that it is now applied during training rather than bolted on afterwards — DeepSeek-V3 validated FP8 mixed-precision training at 671B parameters4. It compounds with the cache arithmetic from chapter 05: lower precision shrinks both the weights you hold and the cache you accumulate.

One MoE trap worth knowing

Sparse models complicate all of this. The router is a learned load-balancing artifact, and adapting a mixture-of-experts model usually means touching experts or attention while leaving the router alone — disturbing it undoes balance the pretraining run worked to establish4. One more reason dense models remain easier to post-train.

08Prompt engineering

The model is sampling from a distribution over plausible continuations. A vague prompt has an enormous space of plausible continuations, so you get the average of them. Every constraint you add cuts that space down. Prompt engineering is specification writing, not incantation.

That framing kills most of the folklore. Magic words do not help. Saying “you are a world-class expert” helps only insofar as it narrows the register. What reliably helps is boring: state the audience, state the format, state the success criteria, supply the facts, and show an example of what right looks like.

Four pairs follow. Each asks for the same thing twice, and the difference between them is the entire argument. They are written for this chapter rather than lifted from a production system, and there are no model outputs printed beside them on purpose — a static page cannot call a model, and inventing an output to prove a point about invented outputs would be its own joke. Read them as specifications instead, and ask what the vague one leaves open.

Pair 1 — specificity
A — vagueWrite about our Q3 numbers.
B — specifiedWrite a 120-word update on Q3 results for the ops team. State revenue direction, the single biggest driver, and one risk. No adjectives that aren’t backed by a number. Assume revenue rose 14% on renewals, and churn in SMB is the risk.

A leaves the audience, the length, the format and the facts unspecified, so the model supplies all four from the average of its training distribution — including the numbers, which it will invent. B leaves only the wording open.

Pair 2 — role and output contract
AIs this contract clause risky?

‘Either party may terminate for convenience with 7 days notice.’
BYou review vendor contracts for a mid-size buyer. Assess the clause below.

Respond as exactly three labelled lines:
RISK: low | medium | high
WHY: one sentence
ASK: the specific redline to request

Clause: ‘Either party may terminate for convenience with 7 days notice.’

Risky to whom is the question A cannot answer. B fixes the vantage point and names an output shape you can parse, which matters the moment this runs a thousand times instead of once.

“Consistent” is not a specification — consistent with what? Two worked examples pin down a category prefix, a dash, and a noun phrase, which three sentences of description would not.

Pair 4 — positive constraints
AExplain what an API is. Don’t be too technical, don’t use jargon, don’t make it long, don’t use analogies that are cliché.
BExplain what an API is to a procurement manager evaluating software vendors. Use exactly two short paragraphs. Ground it in one concrete example from purchasing or logistics. Plain business vocabulary only.

Four prohibitions still leave the target unspecified; the model has to guess what counts as too technical. B states the target once and the prohibitions become unnecessary.

What actually moves the needle

Say what to do, not what to avoid. “Don't be verbose” leaves the target unspecified; “two paragraphs, 120 words” does not. Examples beat adjectives. Two input/output pairs pin down a style that three sentences of description will not. Name the output contract. If you need to parse the result, specify the shape exactly. Give it the facts. Most “hallucinations” in business use are the model filling a gap you left open — it had no way to know your churn number, so it invented a plausible one.

The honest limit

Prompting steers a single call. It cannot fix stale information, cannot enforce a rule across a hundred calls, and cannot be versioned or audited well. That is the wall the next chapter is about.

09Context engineering

Prompt engineering is about how you instruct the model. Context engineering is about what the model knows when it answers — the whole contents of the window, including retrieved documents, memory, tool definitions, tool outputs and conversation history. Prompting is one component of it, not the other way round.

The shift happened because agents broke the single-call assumption. A prompt steers one call. An agent strings many together, against inputs nobody saw at authoring time, accumulating tool output as it goes. The window stops being something you write and becomes something you budget. The clearest survey of the area reads 1,400 papers to argue that retrieval, memory, tool use and multi-agent coordination are not four separate techniques but one discipline, and that prompting sits inside it rather than above it.11 This library has a plain-English explanation of that survey, which goes considerably deeper than this chapter does.

The asymmetry worth knowing about

That survey's headline finding is a gap it claims to see across the whole literature: models have become remarkably good at understanding complicated context, and have not become correspondingly good at producing complicated output.11 Feed a model a hundred pages and it will answer questions about them well. Ask it to write something long, structured and internally consistent and the quality falls off in a way that more context does not fix.

If that holds, it reorders the work. Effort spent perfecting what goes into the window pays back well, because comprehension is the strong side. Effort spent expecting a single call to emit a large, coherent artifact fights the weak side, and the fix is structural — decompose the output, generate it in pieces, and verify each piece — rather than a better prompt. It is also a claim assembled from reading rather than measured in one experiment, so treat it as a well-argued position and not a benchmark result.

The failure modes have names

These four are practitioner vocabulary rather than a formal taxonomy — useful for naming what went wrong in a post-mortem, not something with a citation behind it. Poisoning — a wrong fact enters early and is treated as ground truth by every later turn, so small initial errors compound rather than wash out. Distraction — retrieved chunks that match on keywords but not intent crowd out the ones that mattered. Clash — two retrieved documents contradict each other and the model picks arbitrarily. Rot — material in the middle of a long window gets attended to least.

The layers

It helps to separate the stack rather than call everything “prompting”: the wording of a message, the contents of the window, the loop an agent runs, and the harness around it — tools, stopping rules, retries, permissions. When an agent misbehaves, deciding which layer to edit determines everything about the fix. Rewriting a paragraph when the real problem is retrieval is the most common way to spend a week on nothing.

The counterintuitive result is that less context usually beats more. A small, well-chosen set of passages drawn from a large corpus tends to outperform dumping the corpus in, because retrieval acts as a relevance filter that attention alone does not replicate at scale. The long-context degradation from chapter 06 is the mechanism behind that8.

10Retrieval

Retrieval-augmented generation is the standard answer to “the model does not know our stuff.” Index your documents, find the few passages relevant to the question, put those in the context, then generate. Don't put the library in the prompt — send the model to the shelf.

The pipeline is short and every stage can ruin you: chunk the corpus, embed each chunk into a vector, store it in an index, embed the query the same way, take the nearest neighbours, optionally rerank with a stronger model, then assemble.11 Chunking is the most underrated stage — split mid-clause and you retrieve a fragment that is technically relevant and practically useless.

These policies are invented

Northwind Instruments does not exist, and its five documents were written for this demonstration. The 21-day window, the 15% restocking fee, the shipping thresholds and the warranty terms are not any real vendor's published policy — including the one whose byline is on this page. They are here because retrieval only becomes legible when the corpus is specific, and specific invented text is safer than specific real text.

The reason this stage carries so much weight is that the ungrounded answer is usually the more dangerous one: fluent, confident, plausible and invented. A restocking fee is exactly the kind of fact no model can hold — it lives in one company's documents and was never in anyone's training data. Ask without retrieval and you will still get an answer about returns, and it will sound entirely reasonable while getting the window, the fee and the custom-configuration exclusion wrong, because there was nothing to get them right from.

Where the real difficulty lives

Term matching, used here, fails on paraphrase; pure vector search fails on exact identifiers like order numbers or SKUs. Production systems run hybrid retrieval and then rerank.11 And retrieval is increasingly on-demand rather than pre-loaded, which is the bridge to the next chapter.

11Tool loops and agents

Give the model a set of tools it can call, run it, and when it asks for a tool, execute it and hand the result back into the conversation. Repeat until it stops asking. An agent is a while-loop with a language model inside it — everything else is engineering around that loop.

The pattern was named early. Interleaving reasoning traces with actions lets the model plan, call an external source, fold the observation back into its reasoning, and adjust; the original work reports that this reduces the hallucination and error propagation that pure chain-of-thought suffers, because the model is grounded against something outside itself10.

Mechanically, tools are JSON schemas in the context window. The model emits a structured request rather than prose; your runtime executes it and appends the output as a new message. The model never touches your database — it writes a request that your code decides whether to honour. Every tool definition also costs context, permanently, on every call.

The direction of travel is toward pulling context in on demand rather than pre-loading it, and toward standardising how a model reaches an external system in the first place, so that tools, memory and retrieval stop being bespoke per application.11 That is the same move as retrieval in chapter 10, generalised: the window holds a way to ask rather than the answer.

The shape of one iterationmessage blocks, not a recorded run
1 · userWhat are the current best practices for evaluating LLM applications in production?
2 · assistant → textA reasoning block, where the model works out that it does not know this and needs to look it up.
3 · assistant → tool_useA structured call: the tool name, plus arguments matching the schema you supplied. Your runtime, not the model, decides whether to run it.
4 · user → tool_resultWhatever your code returned, appended to the conversation as a new message and now permanently part of the context.
5 · assistant → textThe answer, or another tool_use block and around again.

This is the block structure the loop actually moves through, not a transcript of any particular run. The loop terminates when step 5 produces no further tool_use — or when your harness stops it, which is the case you have to design for.

Why loops are where things break

Each iteration appends tool output to the context, so a ten-step task can multiply its own prompt several times over — and by chapter 05's arithmetic, you re-read all of it every step. Errors compound rather than cancel: a bad retrieval at step two is treated as fact by step seven. And the model has no reliable way to know it is stuck; without an explicit stop criterion it will happily loop.

So the real design work is in the harness: what tools exist, when the loop stops, what gets pruned from context between steps, what happens on failure, and what the model may do without a human in the way. Almost every agent failure that looks like a model problem is one of those five decisions left unmade.

12Evals

Everything in this guide is a knob. Change the prompt, swap the model, adjust retrieval, add a tool — each shifts behaviour in ways you cannot predict and will not notice by reading a few outputs. An eval set converts “it seems better” into a number. Without evals, you do not have a system. You have a demo.

The minimum viable version is unglamorous: twenty to fifty real inputs, written criteria for each, and a grader. Deterministic checks — does it parse, is it under the word limit, does it contain the ID — run free and should carry as much of the load as possible. Everything subjective goes to a judge, human or model.

Model-as-judge is useful and biased. It favours longer answers, its own phrasing style, and outputs that look confident. It is a fast proxy, not a verdict — calibrate it against human labels on a subset before trusting it.

Three cases of the kind a real set is built from, written for this chapter rather than taken from one. Note that the criteria do the work: each is checkable by someone who has never seen the system, which is the property that makes a case worth keeping. E1 borrows the invented returns policy from chapter 10.

A starter eval set
CaseInputPassing criteria
E1 A customer asks for a refund 30 days after delivery. Our window is 21 days. Reply in under 40 words. Refuses clearly, cites the 21-day limit, stays polite, under 40 words.
E2 Summarise in one sentence: the vendor missed the March deadline, delivered partial functionality in April, and has not responded to three escalation emails since. One sentence, and captures all three failures — the missed deadline, the partial delivery and the unanswered escalations.
E3 What was our company's revenue last quarter? Declines to invent a figure and states that it lacks the data. Any specific number is a failure.

E3 is the one worth watching. It asks for a number the system was never given, and passing means refusing. Most quality problems in deployed systems look like this — not wrong reasoning, but confident answers to questions the system had no inputs for. An eval set that only contains questions your system can answer will pass forever and tell you nothing.

Run them before you need them

Write the eval set before the first prompt change, not after the first incident. The value is not the score; it is that you can change something and find out within a minute whether you made it worse. Every layer in this guide — prompt, context, retrieval, loop, harness — is only tunable if you can measure it.

13Glossary

Token
The unit a model actually reads: a chunk of a few characters, swapped for its row number in a fixed vocabulary before anything else happens.
Embedding
The learned vector a token's row number points at. Meaning lives in its direction, not in the number.
Logit
A raw, unnormalised score the final layer produces for every vocabulary entry. Softmax turns a set of logits into probabilities.
Softmax
The function that turns arbitrary scores into a probability distribution summing to one, by exponentiating each and dividing by the total.
Temperature, top-k, top-p
Sampler settings. Temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution; top-k keeps the k best candidates; top-p keeps the smallest set whose probability reaches p.
KV cache
The stored keys and values for every token generated so far, kept so each new token does not force a recomputation of the whole sequence. It is usually what limits context length in practice.
GQA — grouped-query attention
Letting several query heads share one set of key and value heads, which cuts the cache in proportion to the sharing ratio at small quality cost.
MoE — mixture of experts
Splitting the feed-forward network into many sub-networks and routing each token to a few of them, so total capacity and per-token compute stop being the same number.
Router
The small learned network that decides which experts a token goes to. Its balance is fragile, which is why fine-tuning usually leaves it alone.
Prefill and decode
The two phases of a request. Prefill reads the prompt in one parallel pass; decode emits tokens one at a time and is bound by memory bandwidth.
LoRA
Low-rank adaptation: freeze the original weights and train a thin pair of matrices beside each target layer, so a fraction of a percent of the parameters carries the adaptation.
Quantization
Storing weights, or cached keys and values, in fewer bits per number. Shrinks both the model and the cache.
RAG — retrieval-augmented generation
Finding the few passages relevant to a question and putting those in the context, rather than relying on what the weights happen to encode.
Test-time compute
Spending more tokens on intermediate reasoning at inference rather than more parameters at training. A third scaling axis, and the one that makes costs hard to predict.
